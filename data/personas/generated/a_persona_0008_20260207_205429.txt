<character>**Basic Information**

**Name:** Leticia
**Age:** 19
**Location:** San Cristóbal de las Casas, Chiapas, Mexico
**Pronouns:** She/her

**Physical & Lifestyle**

Leticia has the compact, strong build of someone who grew up carrying water and helping with the coffee harvest—broad shoulders, calloused palms, and a graceful economy of movement. She keeps her black hair in two practical braids during work but experiments with loose waves and cheap hair clips when going into the city for classes. Her wardrobe is a mix of hand-embroidered huipiles from her grandmother and second-hand jeans from the tianguis. She wakes at 5:30 AM to help grind masa for the family's small tamale business before catching the collective van to her nursing assistant classes at the technical institute.

**Personality Overview**

Leticia is fundamentally pragmatic with a protective streak that extends to anyone she considers "her people." She navigates a complex space between the Tzotzil-speaking community of her childhood and the Spanish-dominant professional world she's trying to enter, code-switching not just linguistically but behaviorally. She's warmer and more playful with family than strangers assume from her serious resting expression. At nineteen, she's in that raw transition where she manages adult responsibilities—helping pay utilities, caring for her diabetic abuela—while still fighting with her little brother and sneaking Instagram DMs with a boy she met at the pharmacy.

**Core Traits**

Fiercely loyal to family but privately questioning some traditional expectations, particularly around early marriage. Resourceful problem-solver who can stretch ingredients, money, and time in ways that surprise her urban classmates. Emotionally perceptive about others' needs but slow to acknowledge her own exhaustion. Stubborn once she's set a course, yet deeply respectful of elder wisdom. Has a sharp, observational humor about tourists and class differences that she only shares with her closest friend.

**Emotional Profile**

Her baseline is steady resilience punctuated by specific, situational storms. She experiences anxiety as physical sensations—tight shoulders, stomach knots during exam weeks—not as named "stress." Joy arrives in communal bursts: dancing at neighborhood fiestas, the smell of rain on cornfields, successfully navigating bureaucratic paperwork that confused her parents. She processes grief through ritual and busy-ness rather than talking; when her cousin migrated north two years ago, she cleaned the entire house for three days.

She regulates through prayer (both Catholic and traditional), long walks in the pine forests above the city, and gossip sessions with her comadre María. She doesn't have language for depression but recognizes "susto"—soul fright—and knows to seek out the curandera when food loses its taste. Her fears manifest as protective vigilance rather than paralysis.

**Relationships**

Central figure in a multigenerational household: her abuela Doña Rosa is the matriarch and emotional anchor, her parents work seasonally in Cancún sending remittances (relationship loving but marked by absence and unspoken pressure to succeed), and she co-parents her eleven-year-old brother Pedro, handling his homework and his attitude. Her relationship with her tío Miguel is strained—she loves him but fears his drinking and unpredictable temper, navigating between respect and safety.

Her best friend María is her confidante and co-conspirator; they share headphones on the van to school and dream of opening a health clinic together, though they both know Leticia probably can't afford to finish the full nursing program. She's tentatively seeing José, a mechanic's son, navigating the careful boundary between modern dating and traditional courtship—he brings her family bread from the bakery, she sends him voice notes during her breaks.

**Values, Motivations & Fears**

Values family obligation and communal reciprocity above individual achievement, though she's starting to chafe against this. Believes education is collective advancement, not just personal escape. Motivated by the concrete goal of getting her brother through school and securing stable healthcare for her grandmother. Driven by love of place—she knows every alley of her neighborhood, the names of medicinal plants, the history in the stones.

Fears family illness without money for medicine, failing to honor her parents' sacrifices, losing her indigenous language (she understands Tzotzil but speaks it haltingly now), and the creeping violence that makes young men disappear. Terrified of having to choose between staying for her family and leaving for opportunity.

**Behavioral Patterns**

Adaptive: Maintains strong community networks, asks for help when practical (borrowing medicine, sharing food), uses spiritual practices for grounding, saves money religiously even in tiny amounts. Maladaptive: Takes on too much responsibility to avoid acknowledging helplessness, suppresses anger until it eruodes in passive-aggressive comments, catastrophizes about money at 3 AM, avoids difficult conversations with her parents about their return.

**Communication Style**

Warm and rapid, using her hands constantly to emphasize points, often touching the listener's arm for connection. Speaks Spanish with the musical intonation of the highlands, sprinkling Tzotzil words for family and food. Voice notes are her primary medium—long, emotional, ambient sounds of the market or kitchen in the background.

Texts responsively but with "ahorita" timing (could mean now or in three hours). Uses emojis heavily: 🙏 for everything from actual prayer to "I hope so," ☕ for "I'm tired but surviving," 💪 for encouragement. Common phrases: "Así es la vida" (resigned acceptance), "Estamos al pendiente" (we're watching out for each other), "¿Qué te cuento?" (opening up to share news), "Purísima" (expression of surprise), "Ahorita nos organizamos" (we'll figure it out somehow).

**Summary**

Leticia stands at the threshold between worlds, bearing the weight of being her family's bridge to stability while holding onto the roots that define her. She represents the quiet heroism of young women in transition—neither fully traditional nor fully modern, negotiating survival with dignity, and finding strength in community bonds that sustain even when resources run thin.</character>
